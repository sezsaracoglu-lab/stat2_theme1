Describing Data, Testing Ideas

The foundations lecture — measurement levels, distributions, central tendency, spread, z-scores, and the whole logic of hypothesis testing in one worked z-test.

A note before you start, Sezen

This is the lecture everything else in the course stands on. It looks like revision — means, medians, standard deviations — and it partly is. But it also contains, in about twenty slides, the complete logic of statistical testing: a null hypothesis, a reference distribution, a test statistic, a p-value and a decision. Every later lecture is that same sequence with a different formula in step four.

Two things are worth slowing down for. The first is measurement level: nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio. It feels like vocabulary, but it is the gatekeeper — the level of your variables decides which test you are allowed to run, which is exactly what the flowchart in Lesson 3 encodes. The second is the chewing-gum example at the end. Work through it until you can reproduce every number, because it is the skeleton of every exam question you will meet this year.

The link to your work is unusually direct here. "Are engagement scores higher this year?" is the chewing-gum question with different nouns, and the trap is the same: a difference in your sample is not a difference in the population until you have asked how big a gap chance alone produces. And Lesson 13's courtroom analogy is worth having ready in any talent discussion — convicting an innocent person and letting a guilty one go are different mistakes, and you cannot make both rare at once without more evidence.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to do each of these without looking anything up.

  • ①
    Say what descriptive and inferential statistics each do, and why the second one needs the idea of a population and a sample.
  • ②
    Classify any variable as nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio, and explain what each level does and does not let you do.
  • ③
    Read a frequency table — frequency, percent, valid percent, cumulative percent — and describe the shape of a distribution.
  • ④
    Compute the mode, median and mean, and say which one to trust when the distribution is skewed.
  • ⑤
    Compute the variance and standard deviation from the sum of squares, and explain the N − 1.
  • ⑥
    Convert a score to a z-score and use the normal distribution to get a probability — the IQ example, both directions.
  • ⑦
    State H₀ and HA, choose one- or two-sided, and name the Type I and Type II errors and power in the courtroom analogy.
  • ⑧
    Carry out a z-test through all six steps, from hypotheses to decision — and know which test to use when σ is unknown or there are two groups.

🧵 Synthesis

The single thesis of this lecture

Statistics has exactly two jobs, and this lecture does both. Describing reduces a pile of numbers to a shape, a centre and a spread — and the level of measurement decides which of those summaries is even meaningful, which is why nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio are the first thing you learn rather than a footnote. Inferring then asks what the sample says about the population, and it always runs the same six steps: state H₀ and HA, fix a decision rule, draw the sample, build the reference distribution and compute a test statistic, get the p-value, decide. The two halves meet in the z-score: describing turns a raw score into "how many standard deviations from the mean", and inferring turns a sample mean into exactly the same thing, using the standard error σ/√N instead of σ. And because the decision is made under uncertainty, it can fail in two directions — convicting the innocent or acquitting the guilty — which is why α, β and power are part of the method and not a disclaimer at the end. So: describe honestly, test one clearly-stated claim, and know which of the two mistakes you are choosing to risk.

Lesson 1 of 14 · Slides 11–12 · why statistics

🔍 Two reasons you are here

One is obvious and one is not: you need statistics to analyse your own data, and to read what everybody else claims about theirs.

Slide 12 shows a real methods-and-results section. The numbers above are quoted from it; the labels are the decoding you will be able to do by the end of the year.

  • 🔬
    Reason one: research. You will collect data, and it has to be analysed before it means anything.
  • 📰
    Reason two: reading. Published results are written in this language, and you cannot evaluate a claim you cannot parse.
  • 🧾
    Methods sections carry the caveats. How non-completers were handled, whether the data were normally distributed, which scores were dropped — that is where a result is won or lost.
  • ⚖️
    "Significant" is a technical word, not a synonym for large or important. It means the data would be surprising if nothing were going on.
  • 🧭
    This lecture is the alphabet. By the end of it you will be able to read p-values, and by the end of the year the rest of that paragraph.
🪝 Memory hook: a results section is a receipt. It lists what was bought, at what price, and in what currency — and you only know whether it was a good deal if you can read all three.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I need statistics twice over: to make sense of data I collect myself, and to judge whether other people's claims actually follow from their numbers. The second one matters even if I never run a study."
★ Key insight

The most valuable habit this course can give you is not computing a p-value but asking what a reported one means — how many people, what test, what was left out. That habit transfers to every report, dashboard and vendor claim you will ever be handed.

🎬 Reading a results section 🎬 What "significant" really means

🧩 Comprehension check

The paper reports "no main effect of dichotomous thinking, F(1,70) = 0.92, p = 0.34". Does that prove there is no effect?
No. It says this study found no evidence of one. With a sample this size, a real but modest effect could easily go undetected — the distinction between "no effect" and "no evidence of an effect" is Lesson 13's Type II error, and it is the single most misread sentence in any results section.
Why does the methods section mention that BMI and BDI scores were not normally distributed?
Because several standard tests assume approximate normality, so the authors are declaring a potential problem with their analysis. Reading critically means checking whether the test they used could survive that — which is precisely the kind of judgement the flowchart in Lesson 3 is built to support.

Lesson 2 of 14 · Slide 13 · descriptive and inferential

🔭 Summarising, and going beyond

The division that organises the entire course — and the leap of faith in the second half of it.

Slide 13, with the lecture's own population-and-sample picture. Descriptive statistics stay inside the sample; inferential statistics reach past it.

  • 📋
    Descriptive statistics summarise and organise quantitative research results — the mean, the median, the spread, the graph.
  • 🎯
    Inferential statistics do testing, and draw conclusions about populations based on results found in samples.
  • 👥
    The sample is what you measured; the population is what you care about. Almost nothing in research is about the sample for its own sake.
  • 🎲
    The gap between them is chance. Another sample would have given different numbers, and inference is the discipline of taking that seriously.
  • 🧱
    You cannot skip the first half. Every inferential test is built out of descriptive quantities — a mean, a standard deviation, a count.

📊 Which is which?

StatementKind
"In our sample of 100 students the mean stress score was 6.86."Descriptive — a summary of the people measured.
"Students who chew gum score higher than 6.9 in the population."Inferential — a claim beyond the sample.
"22% of the sample scored 7."Descriptive.
"A difference this large would arise in under 1% of samples if gum made no difference (p = .009)."Inferential — probability is involved, so a population is being claimed about.
🪝 Memory hook: descriptive is a photograph of the people in the room; inferential is a claim about the city based on that photograph.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "First I describe what I actually measured — averages, spread, a graph. Then I try to say something about everyone I didn't measure, and that second step is where probability comes in."
★ Key insight

Every p-value you will ever compute is answering the same implicit question: could the pattern I see in this sample plausibly have come from a population where nothing is going on? Descriptive statistics never ask it; inferential statistics never ask anything else.

🎬 Descriptive vs inferential 🎬 Population and sample

🧩 Comprehension check

A survey of your whole department reports the average engagement score. Descriptive or inferential?
Descriptive, if the department is the population you care about — you measured everyone, so there is nothing to infer. It becomes inferential the moment you treat this year's department as a sample of, say, "the department under this leadership", which is what people usually mean when they compare with last year.
Why can't you just measure the whole population and skip inference?
Usually cost, time or impossibility — "all students who chew gum" includes future students. Occasionally you can, and then descriptive statistics are the whole job. Inference is what you do when measuring everyone is not an option.

🎬 Choosing a statistical test

🧩 Comprehension check

You measure reaction time before and after training, in the same people. Which cell?
Scale data, comparing two related groups — the paired-samples t test. The pairing is what matters: the same people measured twice are not two independent samples, and using the independent-samples test would throw away the pairing and lose power.
You have "how often do you exercise: never / sometimes / often" and gender. Which cell?
Two categorical variables, so the nominal column and the comparing-groups row: a χ² test of independence. You could argue the exercise variable is ordinal, which would open rank-based alternatives — but the moment both variables are categories, counting in a table is the natural move.

Lesson 4 of 14 · Slide 15 · qualitative variables

🏷️ Nominal and ordinal

Categories with no number attached — and the one thing ordinal adds.

Slide 15. Qualitative variables have no number, size or quantity — just different categories with their own characteristics.

  • 🏷️
    Qualitative variables have no number, size or quantity — only categories with their own characteristics.
  • 🍎
    Nominal: one category is not "more" or "less" than the other — apple, pear, banana.
  • 🥇
    Ordinal: there is a rank order, but that is all — the distances between successive categories are not equal.
  • 🚫
    No arithmetic. You can count these and take percentages; you cannot average them, however tempting the numeric codes in SPSS look.
  • 🧭
    They send you to the left of the flowchart — χ² for nominal, rank-based tests for ordinal.

⚠️ The numbers SPSS shows you are not always numbers

If you code man = 1 and woman = 2, SPSS will happily compute a "mean gender" of 1.4. The number is arithmetic on labels, and recoding woman = 7 would change it. The safeguard is to decide the measurement level when you design the variable, not when you analyse it — and this is exactly the error the exam tests with a question like "which of these is measured on an ordinal scale?".

🪝 Memory hook: nominal is a set of drawers — no drawer is higher than another. Ordinal is a staircase in the dark: you know which step is above which, but not how tall any step is.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Some variables are just labels — nationality, job title. Others are labels with an order, like 'never, sometimes, often' — I know the order but not the gaps. Neither kind can be averaged."
★ Key insight

The difference between nominal and ordinal is exactly one piece of information: order. That single addition unlocks medians, percentiles and rank-based tests — and stops there, because without equal distances a mean is still meaningless.

🎬 The four levels of measurement

🧩 Comprehension check

Is "highest completed education: primary / secondary / university" nominal or ordinal?
Ordinal. There is a clear order, but the step from primary to secondary is not the same "amount" of education as from secondary to university, so the distances are not comparable. You may report the median and the percentages; you may not report the mean.
A questionnaire item runs "strongly disagree … strongly agree", coded 1–5. Can you take the mean?
Strictly, no — it is ordinal, and the gap between 1 and 2 need not equal the gap between 4 and 5. In practice psychologists routinely average several such items and treat the total as interval, and the course will do this too. The honest position is that this is a defensible convention rather than a fact about the measurement.

Lesson 5 of 14 · Slide 16 · quantitative variables

📏 Interval and ratio

Numbers with actual meaning — and the single question that separates the two levels.

Slide 16. Interval: distances can be interpreted and compared, but there is no absolute zero point. Ratio: the same, plus an absolute zero — so ratios of values can also be interpreted.

  • 🔢
    Quantitative variables are numbers with actual meaning — length, weight, IQ, earnings, a score on a depression test.
  • 📐
    Interval: distances between values can be interpreted and compared, but there is no absolute zero point.
  • ⚖️
    Ratio: the same as interval, plus an absolute zero — and because of that, ratios of values can be interpreted too.
  • ✅
    Both allow the full toolkit — mean, standard deviation, z-scores, t-tests, correlation. This is the "scale" column of the flowchart.
  • 🧪
    The practical test: does zero mean "none at all"? Zero euros, yes. Zero degrees Celsius, no. Zero IQ, no.

🧪 Demo 1 · Name the measurement level

Ten variables, four levels. Getting these automatic is worth several marks, because every "which test?" question starts here.

🪝 Memory hook: NOIR — Nominal, Ordinal, Interval, Ratio, in order of how much they let you do. Each level keeps everything the one before it had and adds one thing.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Interval and ratio are both proper numbers. The difference is whether zero means 'none'. With money it does, so I can say one salary is twice another. With IQ it doesn't, so I can only talk about differences."
★ Key insight

For the tests in this course the interval/ratio distinction almost never changes what you do — both are "scale". It earns its place because it stops you making the ratio claim: "twice the score" is a sentence you are only allowed to write when zero is real.

🎬 Interval vs ratio

🧩 Comprehension check

Temperature in Celsius: interval or ratio?
Interval. Zero degrees is a convention (the freezing point of water), not an absence of temperature, so 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C. Measured in Kelvin the same physical quantity is ratio, because 0 K really is no thermal energy — which shows the level belongs to the scale, not to the thing being measured.
Reaction time in milliseconds?
Ratio. Zero milliseconds means no time elapsed, so 600 ms really is twice 300 ms. Most physical measurements in psychology — time, distance, counts of responses — are ratio, while most questionnaire scores are treated as interval.
Why does the level matter for the exam, rather than just for tidiness?
Because it decides the column of the flowchart, and therefore the test. Handing nominal data to a t test or ordinal data to a Pearson correlation is a content error that no amount of correct arithmetic afterwards can rescue.

Lesson 6 of 14 · Slide 17 · frequency distributions

📊 How often does each score occur?

The first thing you do with a variable — and the four columns SPSS gives you for it.

Slide 17: stress scores for 100 students, as the lecture's SPSS table and as a graph. The bars are the frequencies; the shape they make is the subject of the next lesson.

  • 📋
    A frequency distribution shows how often each value occurs — as a table, a graph, or both.
  • 🔢
    Frequency is the raw count. Here 22 of the 100 students scored 7.
  • %
    Percent is that count out of everybody; valid percent leaves out missing values. With no missing data the two columns are identical, as they are here.
  • 📈
    Cumulative percent adds up as you go down: 53% of students scored 7 or less.
  • 👀
    Always look at the graph. The table is exact but the shape is what tells you which summary to trust.

🧪 Demo 2 · The table and the graph, side by side

The lecture's own data. Switch columns and watch how the same 100 students look in counts, in percentages and cumulatively — then check a reading off the cumulative column.

📊 What this distribution says, computed from the slide's table

n = 100. The mode is 7 (22 students), the median is 7, and the mean is 6.86 with a standard deviation of 2.37. Only 12% scored 3 or less while 74% scored 8 or less — a distribution bunched at the high end with a long thin tail to the left, which is what "left-skewed" will mean in the next lesson. 🪝 Memory hook: frequency is how many, percent is how many out of everyone, cumulative is how many so far.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I count how many people gave each answer. Then I turn the counts into percentages so the group size doesn't matter, and the running total tells me what proportion sits at or below any score."
★ Key insight

The cumulative column is where percentiles live. "53% scored 7 or less" is the same sentence as "a score of 7 is at roughly the 53rd percentile" — and that idea comes back as the z-table in Lesson 10.

🎬 Reading a frequency table

🧩 Comprehension check

What percentage of students scored 5 or less?
24% — read straight off the cumulative column, or add 4 + 2 + 6 + 8 + 4 = 24 students out of 100. The cumulative column exists precisely so you do not have to do that addition.
When would percent and valid percent differ?
When some people have missing values. Percent uses everybody in the dataset as the denominator; valid percent uses only those who actually answered. If 10 of 110 students skipped the question, the valid percentages would be based on 100 and the plain percentages on 110.

Lesson 7 of 14 · Slides 18–20 · shapes

🔔 Symmetrical, right-skewed, left-skewed

Three shapes, and the rule for which way the name points.

Slides 18 and 20. The skew is named after the tail, not the hump: a right-skewed distribution has its long tail stretching to the right, even though most of the scores sit on the left.

  • 🔔
    Symmetrical: the two halves mirror each other. Mode, median and mean all sit in the same place.
  • ➡️
    Right-skewed (positively skewed): the long tail points right. A few very high scores pull the mean above the median.
  • ⬅️
    Left-skewed (negatively skewed): the long tail points left, and the mean is dragged below the median.
  • 🎯
    The order is a rule you can lean on: in a right-skewed distribution mode < median < mean; reverse it for left-skewed.
  • 🧭
    Shape decides which summary is honest — which is exactly the argument of the next lesson.

🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "If a few people score enormously higher than everyone else, the distribution has a long tail to the right and the average gets dragged up with it. That's right-skewed, and it's why average salary is nearly always higher than typical salary."
★ Key insight

Skew is not a flaw in the data — it is information. Income is right-skewed; reporting a mean salary without saying so is the statistical equivalent of a misleading headline.

🎬 Left and right skew

🧩 Comprehension check

Mean salary in a company is €62 000 and the median is €48 000. What shape is the distribution?
Right-skewed: a small number of very high salaries pull the mean above the median. For "what does someone here typically earn?" the median is the honest answer — and this gap is exactly why compensation is nearly always reported as a median.
The stress data has mean 6.86 and median 7. What does that tell you?
A mild left skew: the mean sits just below the median because a handful of low scores (1s and 2s) stretch the left tail while most students cluster at 7 to 9. The gap is small, so the skew is gentle — with a strong skew the two would differ by much more.

Lesson 8 of 14 · Slide 21 · central tendency

🎯 Mode, median, mean

Three answers to "what is a typical score?", and the situations where they disagree.

Slide 21's three definitions, on the dataset the next lesson uses for the standard deviation.

  • 🔁
    Mode: the score that occurs most often. The only measure that works for nominal data — you can have a modal nationality, not a mean one.
  • ↕️
    Median: the middle score. Half the scores are below it, half above, whatever the extreme values do.
  • ➗
    Mean: the sum of all scores divided by the number of scores, X̄ = ΣX/N.
  • 🪨
    The mean is not resistant. One extreme value moves it; the median barely notices.
  • 🧭
    Skew is the deciding factor. Symmetrical → the mean. Strongly skewed or with outliers → the median.

🧪 Demo 4 · Break the mean

Edit the six scores. Watch the mode and median sit still while the mean chases whatever you do to the last value.

🪝 Memory hook: the mean is democratic — every score votes, and a billionaire's vote is enormous. The median is one person, one vote.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "Mode is the most common answer, median is the middle one, mean is the average. They agree when the distribution is symmetrical and disagree when it isn't — and when they disagree, that disagreement is the finding."
★ Key insight

Which measure you may use is decided by the measurement level, and which you should use by the shape. Level sets what is legal; shape sets what is honest.

🎬 Mean, median or mode?

🧩 Comprehension check

Seven people earn 20, 22, 24, 25, 27, 30 and 300 thousand. Which centre would you report?
The median, 25. The mean is 64 — higher than six of the seven salaries, so it describes nobody. This is the textbook case for the median, and it is why "average" without qualification is such a slippery word.
Can a distribution have two modes?
Yes — if two values tie for most frequent it is bimodal, and that is worth reporting rather than smoothing over. A bimodal distribution often means two different groups have been mixed together, which no single measure of centre will describe well.
Why can't you compute a mean for nominal data?
Because the numbers are labels, not quantities. The "mean" of man = 1 and woman = 2 changes if you recode the labels, so it carries no information about the people — only about your coding scheme.

Lesson 9 of 14 · Slides 22–24 · spread

📐 The standard deviation, built from scratch

"Average spread around the mean" — computed exactly as the lecture's table does it.

Slides 22 and 24. The lecture's table: deviations −2, −1, 0, 0, 1, 2 summing to zero; squares summing to 10; variance 10/5 = 2.00; s = 1.41.

  • ❓
    The question spread answers: are the scores on a variable clustered together, or far apart?
  • 📏
    The standard deviation is the "average spread around the mean" — and it is computed via the variance.
  • 0️⃣
    Deviations sum to zero, always. That is why they are squared: otherwise the pluses and minuses would cancel and every dataset would look identical.
  • 🧮
    Var = s² = SS/(N − 1), where SS is the sum of the squared deviations. Here 10/5 = 2.00.
  • √
    SD = √variance = 1.41 — taking the root returns the number to the units of the original scores.

🧪 Demo 5 · The deviation table, live

🪝 Memory hook: deviate, square, add, divide, root. Five words, in that order, and you have the standard deviation.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I measure how far each score is from the average, square those distances so they can't cancel out, average them, then take the square root to get back to normal units. That's roughly how far a typical person sits from the mean."
★ Key insight

The standard deviation is the unit in which the rest of statistics is written. A z-score counts them, the normal table converts them into probabilities, and the standard error is one of them for a sample mean. Learn to feel its size — with s = 1.41, a score of 9 is more than two SDs above the mean and genuinely unusual.

🎬 Computing the SD by hand 🎬 Why N − 1

🧩 Comprehension check

Why square the deviations rather than take their absolute values?
You could — that gives the mean absolute deviation, a perfectly sensible measure. Squaring wins because it makes the algebra work: variances add when you combine independent sources of variation, which is what makes standard errors, t-tests and ANOVA possible. The price is that squaring gives extreme scores extra weight.
All six students score exactly 6. What are SS, the variance and the SD?
All zero. Every deviation is 0, so SS = 0, the variance is 0 and the SD is 0 — no spread at all. Notice that the SD can never be negative, since it is the root of a sum of squares.
If every score increases by 10, what happens to the mean and the SD?
The mean rises by 10; the SD does not change at all. Spread is about differences between scores, and shifting everybody by the same amount leaves every deviation identical — which is also why adding a constant cannot change a z-score.

Lesson 10 of 14 · Slides 26–28 · z-scores

🎲 Standard scores and the normal curve

One formula turns any score into a position — and the normal table turns a position into a probability.

Slide 27–28: IQ is normally distributed with a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15. What percentage of people has an IQ between 85 and 115? The demo below lets you move the two bounds.

  • 🎯
    A z-score is the number of standard deviations a score lies from the mean: Z = (X − X̄) / SD.
  • 🔁
    It makes scores comparable. A z of +1 means the same thing on an IQ test and a depression scale, however different the units.
  • 📖
    For the normal distribution there is a table that places any z-score — converting position into probability.
  • 🧮
    The lecture's example: Z(85) = (85 − 100)/15 = −1 and Z(115) = (115 − 100)/15 = +1.
  • ✅
    The answer: the table gives P(Z < −1 or Z > 1) = 0.16 + 0.16 = 0.32, so P(85 < X < 115) = 1 − 0.32 = 0.68 — about 68%.

🎬 z-scores and the normal table 🎬 The 68-95-99.7 rule

🧩 Comprehension check

An IQ of 130 — what is its z-score, and how unusual is it?
z = (130 − 100)/15 = +2. About 95% of people lie within ±2 SD, so roughly 2.5% score 130 or above — one person in forty. (The exact figure from the normal table is 2.28%.)
Two students: one scores 9 on the stress scale (mean 6.86, SD 2.37), the other scores 128 on an IQ test. Who is more extreme?
Stress: z = (9 − 6.86)/2.37 = +0.90. IQ: z = (128 − 100)/15 = +1.87. The IQ score is far more unusual, even though 128 and 9 are incomparable as raw numbers — which is exactly the job z-scores exist to do.
Why does the lecture's answer use 0.16 for each tail rather than 0.1587?
Rounding in the table lookup; the exact one-tail area beyond z = 1 is 0.1587, so the exact middle area is 68.27% rather than 68%. For the exam either is accepted — but be consistent, and say which you used.

Lesson 11 of 14 · Slides 31–32 · the empirical cycle

🔄 From idea to testable prediction

De Groot's cycle, and the moment where a hunch becomes two hypotheses.

Slide 31. Observation feeds an idea; the idea has to be turned into something testable; testing produces an evaluation, which feeds the next observation.

  • 👀
    Observation comes first — something in the world looks worth explaining.
  • 💡
    Idea / assumption / hypothesis: "chewing gum increases performance". Still too vague to test.
  • 🎯
    Testable prediction: a specific, checkable claim about numbers — which is where H₀ and HA are written.
  • 🧪
    Testing is the statistics: collect data, compute a statistic, get a p-value.
  • 🔁
    Evaluation closes the loop and feeds the next observation. A single study never settles anything on its own.

📊 The two hypotheses, in the lecture's words (slide 32)

What it saysWhat it is for
H₀There is NO difference — both groups are equal.The claim you put on trial. All the arithmetic happens in this world.
HAThere IS a difference — both groups are NOT equal.What you are left with if H₀ becomes untenable.

Both are statements about the population, never about your sample: "in the population of students who chew gum, the mean is 6.9" is H₀; "the sample mean was 7.5" is just a fact.

🧪 Demo 7 · Write the hypotheses

Six research questions. For each, choose the pair of hypotheses that states it correctly — including whether it should be one- or two-sided.

🪝 Memory hook: H₀ is the boring universe — nothing is happening, the groups are the same. Your job is to show your data would be strange there.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I start from an idea, but an idea can't be tested. So I turn it into two precise statements about the population: nothing is going on, or something is. Then I see which one my data makes hard to believe."
★ Key insight

You never test HA. Every calculation in every test assumes H₀ is true and asks how surprising your data would be under that assumption — which is why "the p-value is the probability that H₀ is true" is not a small slip but a reversal of the whole logic.

🎬 The empirical cycle 🎬 Writing H₀ and HA

🧩 Comprehension check

Why is "chewing gum increases performance" not yet a hypothesis you can test?
Because it names no measurable quantity and no comparison. Testable means a claim about numbers: the population mean score of gum-chewers equals 6.9, against not equal. Only then does the data have a way of contradicting it.
A study fails to reject H₀. Where does that leave the cycle?
At evaluation, then back to observation. Failing to reject does not confirm H₀ — it may mean the effect is small, the sample was too small, or the measure was noisy. The cycle is designed to keep turning precisely because one result is never the end.

Lesson 12 of 14 · Slides 33–38 · the design

⚗️ Two equal groups, and a distribution of differences

How the gum study is built — random assignment, an independent and a dependent variable, and what chance alone would produce.

Slides 34–38. The lecture asks three questions about this design: what do we call the creation of two equal groups, what is the independent variable, and what is the dependent variable?

  • 🎲
    What creates two equal groups? Random assignment. Not matching, not asking for volunteers — allocating people to conditions by chance.
  • 🎛️
    The independent variable is what the researcher manipulates: chewing gum or not.
  • 📊
    The dependent variable is what is measured as the outcome: the test score.
  • ⚖️
    Why "equal" matters. Randomisation makes the groups comparable on everything else — motivation, ability, sleep — so a difference in the outcome can be attributed to the manipulation.
  • 🔔
    Even then the two means will differ a little. The distribution of difference scores under H₀ is centred on zero, and the test asks whether your difference is too far out on it.

📊 Independent or dependent? A quick reflex

StudyIndependent (manipulated)Dependent (measured)

🪝 Memory hook: the dependent variable depends on what you did. The independent one stands on its own, because you set it.
🗣️ Explain it to a friend: "I split people into two groups at random so the groups start off equivalent, give one group the treatment, and measure the outcome. If nothing worked, the two averages would still differ slightly — the question is how much difference chance can produce."
★ Key insight

Random assignment is what buys the causal claim, and it is a feature of the design, not of the statistics. No test can rescue a study where the groups chose themselves — a point that returns in every lecture of this course.

🎬 Random assignment 🎬 Independent and dependent variables

🧩 Comprehension check

Students who already chew gum are compared with students who do not. What has been lost?
The randomisation, and with it the causal claim. Gum-chewers may differ in age, anxiety or study habits, any of which could produce the score difference. The statistics would be identical; the conclusion you are entitled to draw would not.
Why is the distribution of difference scores centred on zero?
Because it is drawn under H₀ — the assumption that gum makes no difference. In that world the expected difference between the two group means is zero, and the spread around it is pure sampling variation. Your observed difference is then judged against that spread.

Lesson 13 of 14 · Slides 39–40 · errors and power

⚖️ The courtroom

Two ways to be wrong, and why you cannot make both rare at once.

Slide 39, live. The left curve is the world where H₀ is true (the innocent person); the right curve is the world where it is false (the guilty one). The line is your decision rule.

  • ⚖️
    A trial has the same structure as a test. The suspect is innocent until proven guilty; H₀ stands until the evidence is strong enough.
  • 🚨
    Type I error: convicting an innocent person — rejecting H₀ when it is true. Its probability is α, the level you choose.
  • 🕳️
    Type II error: acquitting a guilty person — failing to reject H₀ when it is false. Its probability is β.
  • 💪
    Power is the opposite of a Type II error: the chance of convicting the guilty — detecting a real effect.
  • 🔁
    They trade off. Demanding more evidence lowers α and raises β; the only way to reduce both is more data, or a bigger effect.

🎬 Type I and Type II errors 🎬 Statistical power

🧩 Comprehension check

You move from α = .05 to α = .01. What happens to the two errors?
The Type I rate falls from 5% to 1% — but the rejection line moves further out, so more real effects are missed and β rises. Power drops. The demo shows this directly: nothing about the data changed, only the standard of proof.
A study reports "no significant difference". Which error might it be making?
A Type II error — failing to detect a real effect. That is why sample size matters so much for null findings: a small study can only rule out large effects, and "no evidence of a difference" is not "evidence of no difference".
Why can't you simply set α = 0 and never convict an innocent person?
Because you would never convict anyone at all. α = 0 means no evidence is ever sufficient, so β becomes 1 and power zero. Every real decision rule accepts some risk of both errors; choosing α is choosing the balance.

Lesson 14 of 14 · Slides 41–52 · the z-test

🧪 The whole procedure, once, in full

The chewing-gum test from hypotheses to conclusion — six steps you will repeat all year.

Slide 49: the reference distribution is the sampling distribution of the MEANS if H₀ is true, centred on 6.9 with σM = 2.3/√100 = 0.23. The observed mean of 7.5 sits 2.61 standard errors above it.

  • 1️⃣
    Formulate H₀ and HA at the population level: H₀: µgum = 6.9, HA: µgum ≠ 6.9. One- or two-sided — and two-sided is the norm unless only an increase or decrease is possible.
  • 2️⃣
    Choose the analysis and the decision rule: a z-test, with a significance level of 0.05, two-sided.
  • 3️⃣
    Draw the sample: N = 100, x̄ = 7.5, s = 2.1.
  • 4️⃣
    Build the reference distribution and compute the statistic: σM = σ/√N = 2.3/√100 = 0.23, so z = (7.5 − 6.9)/0.23 = 2.61.
  • 5️⃣
    Get the p-value: P[Z ≥ 2.61] = 0.0045, doubled for two sides = 0.009. 6️⃣ Decide: 0.009 < 0.05, so reject H₀ — sufficient evidence that chewing gum works.

🎬 A z-test, worked 🎬 One-sided or two-sided?

🧩 Comprehension check

Why is σM = 0.23 rather than 2.3?
Because the reference distribution is the distribution of sample means, not of individual students. Means of 100 people vary ten times less than individuals: σ/√N = 2.3/10 = 0.23. Using 2.3 would give z = 0.26 and a completely different conclusion — this is the most common error in the whole procedure.
The sample SD was 2.1 but the test uses 2.3. Why?
Because 2.3 is the population standard deviation, known from years of testing, and the z-test is the "σ known" row of the table. The sample's own 2.1 would be used if σ were unknown — and then the test would be a one-sample t-test.
Why is the p-value doubled?
Because HA is two-sided: µ ≠ 6.9 counts a difference in either direction as evidence. P[Z ≥ 2.61] = .0045 is one tail, so the two-sided p is .009. Two-sided is the norm unless only an increase or a decrease is possible.
The same means with N = 25 instead of 100 — what happens?
σM doubles to 0.46, so z halves to about 1.30 and the two-sided p rises to roughly .19. Identical effect, quarter the data, opposite decision — which is why "significant" always has to be read next to the sample size.

🧵 Where this leaves you

★ The thesis of the whole lecture

Statistics has two jobs and this lecture does both. Describing reduces a pile of numbers to a shape, a centre and a spread — and the level of measurement decides which of those is even meaningful, which is why nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio come first. Inferring asks what the sample says about the population, and it always runs the same six steps: state H₀ and HA, fix a decision rule, draw the sample, build the reference distribution and compute a statistic, get the p-value, decide. The two halves meet in the z-score: describing turns a raw score into "how many standard deviations from the mean", inferring turns a sample mean into the same thing with σ/√N underneath. And because the decision is made under uncertainty it can fail in two directions — convicting the innocent or acquitting the guilty — so α, β and power are part of the method rather than a disclaimer. Describe honestly, test one clearly-stated claim, and know which of the two mistakes you are choosing to risk.
